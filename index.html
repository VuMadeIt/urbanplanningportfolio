About Liam

  • Based in Toronto, Canada
  • Studying since 2024
  • Reach him liamvuplanning@gmail.com

Liam Vu is an award-winning planning student at TMU and a Council Director at the Ontario Professional Planners Institute.

He found planning in grade 12, when a developer came to his high school in Downsview to ask students what the old airport lands should become. Today he explains city-building to residents in person and to thousands of people online.

Outside work and school you will find him volunteering with advocacy groups, at networking events, painting outdoors, learning K-pop choreography, or playing with his two-year-old brother, Duncan.

    Olivia Chow

    Mayor of Toronto · elected 2023 · re-elected 2026

    The Olivia Chow Shrine

    Nathan Phillips Square, after hours. A tribute to Toronto’s mayor, re-elected on October 26, 2026 with 54 per cent of the vote, from a planning student who told everyone to vote.

    What she has done for the city

    Olivia Chow was first elected in 1985, as a school trustee. She spent 12 years on city council and represented Trinity–Spadina in Parliament, became mayor in 2023, and won a second term in October 2026 with 532,201 votes. Here is the planning case for her, the way Liam would make it.

    Housing

    Homes
    for people

    Homes
    for people

    65,000 rent-controlled homes in the plan

    HousingTO plan, raised September 2023

    The plan

    Two months into her term, council raised the city’s target from 40,000 affordable homes to 65,000 rent-controlled homes: 6,500 rent-geared-to-income, 41,000 affordable and 17,500 rent-controlled market homes, within the 285,000 new homes Toronto pledged by 2031. Staff named 51 city-owned sites to deliver 16,000 to 17,500 of them.

    Why planners care

    • Puts rent-controlled and affordable homes in the city’s own plan, not left to the market.
    • Uses public land for housing near transit.

    Streets

    Local
    streets

    Local
    streets

    19 km of protected bike lanes she fought for

    Bloor, Yonge and University, 2024 to 2026

    The fight

    When a 2024 provincial law ordered the lanes on Bloor Street, Yonge Street and University Avenue removed, she argued that local streets should be decided locally, and negotiated for fixes that bring back a car lane without taking away safe cycling, as on Bloor West in Etobicoke. A court blocked the removal in July 2025; the Court of Appeal reversed that in August 2026, and cyclists have asked the Supreme Court of Canada to hear the case.

    Why planners care

    • Safe streets for people on bikes, on the city’s busiest corridors.
    • Local streets decided locally.

    Transit

    Riding
    the TTC

    Riding
    the TTC

    3 years of frozen TTC fares

    City and TTC budgets, 2024 to 2026

    The budget

    All three of her budgets held fares at 2023 prices while adding service; the 2025 budget added the most service in a decade. From September 2026, fare capping makes every ride after the 47th in a month free, dropping to 40 rides in 2027.

    Why planners care

    • Transit people can afford is how a city grows without more traffic.

    Her record, for planners

    What changed for housing, transit, streets and the city’s ability to pay for them in her first term, checked against the city’s, the province’s and the TTC’s own records.

    65,000 rent-controlled homes

    In September 2023 council raised the HousingTO target from 40,000 affordable homes to 65,000 rent-controlled ones, including 6,500 rent-geared-to-income homes, as part of the 285,000 new homes the city pledged by 2031.

    Federal housing money

    On December 21, 2023, she and the prime minister announced $471 million from the Housing Accelerator Fund, for about 11,780 more homes over three years than were already expected.

    A public builder

    Toronto Builds, adopted in May 2025, keeps city land in public hands on long leases, makes at least 30 per cent of the homes affordable for at least 40 years, and puts one Housing Development Office in charge. City-led projects started 3,606 homes in 2025.

    The New Deal with Ontario

    Her November 2023 deal with the province brought up to $1.2 billion in operating support over three years, including $330 million to run the Eglinton and Finch West lines, and set the Gardiner Expressway and the Don Valley Parkway to become provincial highways in fall 2027, freeing about $1.9 billion for transit, housing and city facilities over the next decade.

    A budget that pays its way

    She inherited a $1.8 billion gap. Her 2024 budget found $620 million in savings and raised the residential property tax by 9.5 per cent, including 1.5 per cent for the City Building Fund, rather than cut transit, shelters or programs. By 2026 the increase was down to 2.2 per cent.

    TTC fares held

    Fares have stayed at 2023 prices through the 2024, 2025 and 2026 budgets, while service went up each year. Monthly fare capping started in September 2026.

    Bike lanes defended

    She opposed the province’s 2024 law to remove the Bloor, Yonge and University lanes and kept negotiating for options that keep cyclists safe. The Court of Appeal sided with the province in August 2026; cyclists have asked the Supreme Court to hear the case.

    Empty homes taxed

    With her backing, council voted 21 to 2 in 2023 to triple the Vacant Home Tax, from 1 to 3 per cent of a home’s assessed value from the 2024 tax year, with the money going to housing.
